# Ballotpedia Bias Taxonomy — Review Reference

This is a concise operational digest of the 25 categories in the supplied **Ballotpedia: How we categorize bias** snapshot. Use the bundled PDF for the source wording.

1. **Changes to the status quo** — Be careful not to magnify or legitimize a proposal merely by highlighting a change that was not already prominent in the underlying debate.
2. **Cherry-picking** — Selecting facts or evidence that support one position while ignoring relevant evidence that cuts against it.
3. **Coatrack bias** — Allowing a secondary topic to become the real focus of an article so that the stated subject is obscured or associated with an undue positive/negative connotation.
4. **Editorializing** — Language that communicates the writer's own view while presenting news or facts.
5. **Explicit recommendation** — Directly encouraging the reader to take an action, support a candidate or measure, or oppose a policy.
6. **Factive verbs** — Attribution verbs that presuppose the truth of a claim. They may be appropriate for verifiable facts but can bias treatment of opinions or disputed assertions. Prefer neutral attribution such as `said`, `stated`, or `wrote` when needed.
7. **Failure to capture competing narratives** — Omitting a meaningful competing account of a contested issue, creating bias by omission.
8. **Ideological labeling** — Applying labels such as conservative, liberal, or progressive without sufficient care, context, or acknowledgment of disagreement about the label.
9. **Inappropriate descriptors** — Adjectives or adverbs that add judgment, imprecision, wordiness, or an unsupported characterization rather than stating the underlying facts.
10. **Labeling bias** — Using evaluative labels such as expert or extremist, or applying labels unevenly across comparable actors.
11. **Loaded language** — Wording with emotional or evaluative connotations beyond its literal meaning that may sway the reader.
12. **Overreach in partisan affiliation** — Treating a person as affiliated with a party when the relationship is indirect or insufficiently established. Add context when affiliation is less direct than candidacy or officeholding.
13. **Parroting the news** — Reproducing a source outlet's framing or bias rather than independently researching and presenting the relevant facts and competing narratives.
14. **Photo selection bias** — Choosing images that create an unnecessary positive or negative impression, such as unflattering photographs that make a subject look foolish or threatening.
15. **Placement bias** — Inappropriately highlighting or obscuring information through where it appears in an article or page.
16. **Prediction bias** — Predictive wording that implies momentum, inevitability, energy, or a future result not established by the evidence.
17. **Protagonist selection** — Tone or narrative direction that implicitly casts one actor as the protagonist, often at the expense of other candidates, officials, or groups.
18. **Scare quotes** — Quotation marks used to signal skepticism, sarcasm, or distance from a word or phrase. If the quotation marks function like `so-called`, remove or rephrase them.
19. **Source selection bias** — Relying disproportionately on sources aligned with one party or ideology, which can also lead to cherry-picking or story-selection effects.
20. **Speech tribe terminology** — Terms or phrases associated predominantly with one ideological or partisan community. Avoid them in Ballotpedia's own voice when a neutral descriptive alternative exists; attribute or define them when source usage is relevant.
21. **Star-maker bias** — Repeating media claims about a person's importance, prominence, or influence rather than grounding attention in facts, thereby amplifying someone beyond their demonstrated relevance.
22. **Story selection bias** — Choosing stories or subtopics that consistently favor one person, party, or position while omitting comparable stories that would complicate that impression.
23. **Unclear attribution** — Quoting or paraphrasing material without a clear line to its source, potentially making the writer appear to endorse it as fact.
24. **Unsubstantiated news** — Reporting rumors, hearsay, allegations, threats of legal action, or similarly weakly supported claims without sufficient evidence or specificity.
25. **Weasel words** — Vague assertions that imply evidence or consensus without naming or supplying it, such as `many experts say` or `research has shown` when no support is provided.

## How to apply the taxonomy

- Flag the **specific sentence or structural choice**, not the writer's motives.
- Distinguish a direct textual problem (`MUST FIX`) from a risk that requires surrounding sources or corpus context (`NEEDS CONTEXT`).
- Do not use the taxonomy to force artificial symmetry. Competing narratives should be represented when they are materially supported and relevant.
- Several categories can overlap. Prefer the most specific category and mention a secondary category only when it adds useful editorial guidance.
